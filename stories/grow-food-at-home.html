What if your home is where your food grows?

Ask your children to draw a pizza. They can. Now ask them to draw the tomato they find on the pizza. They might. Now ask them to draw the plant that produced its tomatoes. We bet you that they do not know if tomatoes grow on a tree, on a vine or on a plant.

Wait, we are not being judgemental. We understand. The pizza arrives at your door. The tomato plant makes zero effort at customer acquisition.

Somewhere between growing food and Swiggy, we have lost sight of a beautiful process. Sunlight becomes a little leaf. Water carries nutrients up from the roots into the plant. Something alive emerges that then keeps us alive.

What we see and our children encounter, is a steaming, branded cardboard box. What if a home could bring part of that story back into the home?

Home is where the farm is.

At Urna, food is one of the systems around which we design our apartments. Alongside water, air, energy and space, food should be what the home provides, much like it used to be for our ancestors.

At 71P/Urna and 408A/Urna, the vertical hydroponic farms gives that idea an architectural form. The garden climbs with the building.

Hydroponics allows plants to grow without soil, receiving nutrients through water supplied to their roots. In a recirculating system, unused water and nutrients can be collected and returned, reducing waste. Suitable greens and herbs can be grown close to the people who will eat them.

The vertical farm will not replace the countryside. Nobody should expect the building to supply its own wheat harvest, mango orchard and the occasional confused buffalo.

It can, however, shorten the farm-to-home journey for some of the food on your plate. It can make growing conditions more visible. And it can restore the acquaintance between a home and a living plant (beyond being prints on the bedsheet).

What our vertical farms accomplish.

All of us have become great and knowledgeable commentators on the condition of the planet. But a plant will not thrive because we have expressed politically correct views about sustainability.

A plant needs care. A vertical farm needs suitable crops, clean water, balanced nutrients, working pumps and people who understand the system. Hydroponics does not automatically make produce organic or eliminate the need for responsible crop management. The system creates possibilities, good operation helps us harvest more than tomatoes.

Urna’s dream is to make these possibilities part of the home’s everyday life. Imagine a child watching a leaf develop before it becomes a rocket leaf and goes into her salad. Imagine residents understanding what goes into the greens on the table. Imagine neighbours finding something more engaging to discuss than the one clown every apartment has who parks awfully.

The relationship with food becomes a little more immediate. And perhaps the relationship with the building changes too. The balcony, the facade and the shared garden acquire another purpose. Architecture begins to participate in the life it shelters.

The Urna difference.

We have spent decades asking how life fits inside a building. Urna would like to ask what the building can contribute to life.

The Urna difference

Sometimes the answer will involve careful engineering and an elaborate understanding of agriculture, irrigation and pipes. And sometimes, it will be the fascination of a child plucking a leaf for lunch.
