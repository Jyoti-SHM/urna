Almost all of us know these lines, Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink. But almost none of us will remember that this is from Coleridge’s The Ancient Mariner.

Mumbai should understand the irony. We live beside the sea. We organise entire seasons around the rain. When it rains it pours, it floods, it disrupts life for a few days and it runs away to the sea. But yet, we have a water scarcity.

We turn on a tap, water appears effortlessly. But what we don’t realise is that this simple act of turning on a tap and getting water depends upon a long journey of collection, storage, treatment and distribution.

A home that takes this journey for granted is placing considerable faith in things beyond its boundary. Rain must arrive where it is needed. Infrastructure must keep working. Supply must keep pace with demand.

And all of us are being foolish if we believe the tap will perpetually keep delivering water.

Understanding water at Urna.

How much of our water security can we take responsibility for ourselves?

Our mythology understood this better than we do. Bhagiratha’s effort brought Ganga towards the earth, but someone as powerful as Shiva had to receive and temper her descent. Even a celestial river required somebody to think about how it would arrive. We could apply at least a little of this attention to our plumbing.

In a conventional building, water’s career is very brief. It arrives clean, performs one task and leaves as waste. The same water comes into our water-purifier, into our toilet bowl, into our shower, into our washing machine. And all of it exits as waste, unfortunately. Even when some of it could have further use, with treatment.

This is just incredibly expensive and incredibly stupid.

Most of you would be familiar with India’s old stepwells. Their architecture and the descent and ascent you had to make, acknowledged its importance. We have made access to water more convenient, but convenience cannot make us forget what the importance of what we are accessing.

Water independence at Urna means reducing dependence, making each supply go further and preparing the community to cope better when external sources are under pressure.

How Urna conserves water.

Urna’s approach brings collection, treatment, storage and reuse into the design of the community. Rainwater can be harvested. Separately collected wastewater can be treated and reused for appropriate uses, allowing freshwater to be reserved for the purposes that need it. Water-efficient fittings reduce unnecessary consumption.

Why can’t water used for bathing, after suitable treatment, be reused? Why can’t treated wastewater support landscaping? This calls for engineering, testing and maintenance. This calls for separate plumbing. This call for a lot of safeguards. But in the long run, this means a smaller water bill.

No building can guarantee endless supply. We are dependent on the planet’s whims, too. But we can avoid wasting water and encourage conservation and re-use. See how we do it here.

The Urna difference.

At Urna, we would like the security of a home to extend beyond its locks and cameras. It should also reside in the care with which the building receives, uses and preserves the things life depends upon.
