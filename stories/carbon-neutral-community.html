A building starts leaving its footprint on the planet long before residents start leaving their footprints in their homes.

There is the cement that pours into its foundations. The steel that holds your home upright. The glass, the stone, the pipes. The transport, the machinery. That list is long.

By the time the first resident arrives with a little Ganesha, a large pandit, milk and a coconut, the building already has a substantial history of emissions. And then, pooja done, we switch on the air- conditioning.

This is why a carbon-neutral community requires considerably more thought than a green facade, biophilic architecture and the photograph of a happy child planting a tree. And of course, greeeeeen-looking elevations.

Carbon-neutral living demands that we account for embodied carbon, the emissions associated with materials and construction — the cement, the glass, the steel, all of that. And then, operational carbon, the emissions from running our homes, year after year — the electricity, cooling, heating, lighting, hot water and other building systems. A building has to address both if its carbon-neutral ambition extends across its life.

Accounting for the 2 carbons.

At Urna, the purpose of design is to find ways to account for both, embodied and operational carbon. At Urna our manifesto states that sustainability is not an amenity. Or a marketing claim. Or an option. It is the foundation of responsible urban development.

Before asking how much electricity a building can generate, we ask how much it needs. Can daylight do the work of a light bulb? Can shade reduce the heat entering a room? Can ventilation help keep it comfortable before a machine is asked to intervene?

The sun is already working. So is the wind. So is the rain. And all of them are free. It would be extremely stupid management, to not to give them a job.

Material choices matter too. Recycled steel, reclaimed materials and alternatives that reduce the demand for newly extracted resources can help lower the footprint carried into a building. Our gate at 71P/Urna, for example, is designed from discarded railway sleepers. For more than a hundred years, these Burma-teak sleepers have had thousands of trains and millions of people pass over them. And now, they are happy sentinels to a new world that will walk in past them. See more on our material choices here.

Make the right choices.

A solar panel cannot retrospectively make the emissions of a ton of cement disappear. Nor can a tree. We have to make the right choices every step of the way. Take a look at how we harness solar energy at Urna here.

If you read your Amar Chitra Katha, you would remember Chitragupta, who keeps an account of our deeds. Good intentions do not count, only good deeds do. Construction could do with similar scrutiny — we need to keep note of the emissions they inherit from construction, those they generate every day and those they will leave for the future. At Urna, we would rather have engineers keep account than leave the matter to a divine audit.

Urna’s carbon-neutral ambition, including the community envisioned at 71P/Urna, begins with this willingness to count. Reduce emissions first. Measure what remains. Address the balance through credible, verifiable measures, with a clear account of what is included and over what period.

The word neutral must survive constant audits. And the task is not just the builder’s, it passes on to the residents after possession. Systems need maintenance. Consumption needs monitoring.

Residents need to understand how their choices affect the entire community and a shared goal. A community cannot outsource its conscience to the roof and assume the solar panels will handle everything.

But wait, living responsibly should not become a daily endurance test! The building should make conscious choice easier and luxurious — more comfortable rooms, efficient services and infrastructure that quietly does its work.

The Urna difference.

So, can a community become carbon-neutral?

Yes, provided it treats neutrality as a responsibility to be measured and demonstrated.

This is the community Urna wants to build, beginning with 71P/Urna. One whose residents inherit a beautiful home, and whose children inherit a beautiful world.
