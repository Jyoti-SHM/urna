Ever wondered where your home ends?

If you said it ends at the boundary, we would say that you have a good knowledge of property rights. But at the same time, you are wrong by a distance.

Your water comes from the Western Ghats. Your electricity is generated in tomboy and other thermal stations supplying the national grid. The materials in your walls come from across the country. And the trash you leave outside your home continues travelling long after you have digested its contents.

The consequences of your home and your life extend way farther than you think.

Consider your floor. You encounter it at the end of its story, after it is cut, polished and installed. Before that came the quarrying, the processing, the transport. At Urna, this entire journey is taken into account.

Material choices.

Our concrete blocks, for example, are resurrected from construction debris. They are bigger and stronger than the regular concrete bricks. And more powerful and longer lasting, like the resurrected Christ.

Reclaimed wood, recycled steel and other suitable recovered materials allow existing resources to enter another useful life. Careful sourcing can reduce the demand for new extraction.

These choices require judgement. A material still has to perform its task safely and well. Its environmental merits have to survive questions about where it came from and what will happen to it next. See what we choose here.

After material, people.

The same attention to material extends to the people whose build our homes.

Take a look at the Auriga case study to see how our approach extends sustainability to construction workers through health checks, attention to food and water and educational activities. That widens the meaning of a responsibly built home. The lives involved in making it cannot disappear from the account once the scaffolding comes down.

Parasparopagraho jīvanām is an ancient Jain principle that is Urna’s starting point in its understanding of life through mutual dependence. What sustains us is bound up with what sustains other lives.

Water saved within a community reduces its demand on a shared supply. Waste handled responsibly leaves less for somebody else to manage. A building that consumes fewer resources places fewer demands upon the systems around it.

The Urna difference.

Urna’s ambition asks the home to take greater responsibility. To consume thoughtfully, reuse what can be reused, recover what has value and understand what must leave.

The House of Mittal’s history gives this ambition a context. Having helped shape Mumbai’s growth, our next task is to consider what the city now needs from growth.

Every new building will join a neighbourhood, draw upon shared resources and remain long enough to influence the lives of people who never bought an apartment in it. Its responsibilities and obligations begin before possession and extend beyond ownership.

The Urna difference

Where, then, does an Urna home end? Now that you know, not at the wall, for sure.
